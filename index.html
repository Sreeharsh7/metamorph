An interactive guide to fold switching

One sequence,
two folds.

Most proteins fold into a single shape. A few have two, and switch between them when the cell asks. Scroll to watch real structures change.

scroll ↓

01

The rule

In the 1960s Christian Anfinsen showed that a protein's amino-acid sequence holds everything it needs to fold. Unfold ribonuclease, let it go, and it finds the same structure again. The working rule of structural biology followed: one sequence, one fold.

On the right are 42 residues from the bacterial transcription factor RfaH, folded the way a crystal structure shows them: two α-helices packed side by side.

02

The exception

Keep scrolling. The same 42 residues unwind and refold into a five-stranded β-barrel. Both shapes are real, and both were solved experimentally (PDB 2OUG and 2LCL).

The colours follow secondary structure as it is re-assigned on every frame: helix, strand and loop. Residues that barely move are dimmed, so the part that switches stands out.

α-hairpin

Proteins that do this are called fold switchers or metamorphic proteins. Porter and Looger estimated in 2018 that 0.5–4% of proteins in the PDB may switch folds.

03

Two wells, one barrier

A fold switcher sits on a free-energy landscape with two valleys, one for each fold, separated by a barrier. The protein rests in one valley. Something has to push it over.

No trigger: the left fold is stable.

A trigger, such as a binding partner, temperature, salt or light, does two things. It tilts the landscape so the other fold becomes more stable, and it often lowers the barrier between them. Notice that the protein switches late and switches back late. In the middle range it stays wherever it already was, so a two-well protein can remember its recent past.

04 · E. coli

RfaH: a switch that changes jobs

RfaH controls the transcription of long operons. Most of the time its C-terminal domain is an α-helical hairpin, packed against its own N-terminal domain, which keeps it switched off.

Autoinhibited: the CTD stays helical.

When RNA polymerase pauses at an ops DNA sequence, the N-terminal domain binds it and lets go of the CTD. Freed, the domain refolds into a β-barrel that binds ribosomal protein S10, coupling transcription to translation. Released again, it slowly folds back.

05 · cyanobacteria

KaiB: a switch that keeps time

Three proteins, KaiA, KaiB and KaiC, make a 24-hour clock that keeps running in a test tube. KaiB's ground-state fold is the common one. A rarer, thioredoxin-like fold binds phosphorylated KaiC at night.

Day: ground state.

The switch is slow, taking hours rather than milliseconds. That slowness is not a flaw: the delay it builds into the cycle helps set the clock's period. The animation here is sped up enormously.

06 · human

Lymphotactin: two folds at once

The chemokine lymphotactin (XCL1) is the most even-handed fold switcher known. Under physiological conditions it spends roughly equal time in two unrelated folds, swapping back and forth on a timescale of about a second.

Ltn10 50%Ltn40 50%

Ltn10, the classic chemokine fold, is favoured by cold and salt and activates the receptor XCR1. Ltn40, an all-β dimer fold, is favoured by warmth and low salt and binds heparin. It does two jobs because it has two shapes. The populations shown follow those trends; they are not fitted to data.

07 · human

Mad2: a switch with a catalyst

Before a cell divides, the spindle checkpoint makes sure every chromosome is attached. Mad2 is at its heart. Its open fold (O-Mad2) can close a "safety belt" around Cdc20, giving the closed fold (C-Mad2) that blocks anaphase.

On its own the conversion takes hours, far too slow for the checkpoint. The template model explains how the cell speeds it up: closed Mad2 bound to Mad1 at unattached kinetochores grabs open Mad2 and catalyses its closing. The AAA+ ATPase TRIP13, with p31comet, spends ATP to pry it open again.

08

How the morph is drawn

Experiments give the two end states, not the path between them. The obvious shortcut is to slide every atom in a straight line from one structure to the other. Try it.

closest Cα–Cα approach: –

With straight lines, the chain passes right through itself; red marks Cα atoms closer than 3 Å. Metamorph instead moves each atom a fraction of an ångström at a time toward its target. Virtual bonds are held at 3.8 Å and atoms are kept from overlapping, so strands slide around each other. Where a segment has to change sides, as in Mad2, it lifts off the surface, swings around the core and docks. All four paths are checked so that no two chain segments come closer than about 2.9 Å.

This is an illustration of a physically allowed path, not a simulation of the real one. Real switches often go through partly unfolded states that experiments are only starting to resolve.

09

Why it matters

Fold switchers sit right on the fault line of modern structure prediction. Tools like AlphaFold2 learn one structure per sequence, and for most fold switchers they predict only one of the two folds. Finding the proteins that switch, and predicting both of their shapes, is an open problem.

That is the question behind my research on fold-switching proteins.
